Charging 5,000 electric cars through unreliable systems: what does it cost?

Good control software rescued almost all of it. Against made-up failure rates, unreliable car and charger systems cost about £0.106 per car per month and no car was left uncharged, but the software had to send 1.85 times as many messages to get there.

  • 97.34%of the possible savings kept (with perfect systems: 97.59%)
  • £0.106lost per car per month because the systems are unreliable
  • 1.85xas many messages needed: 7.35 per car per night, against 3.97 with perfect systems

5,000 simulated cars over 30 nights of September 2026, run three times. Every car was charged in time: no missed departures. The failure rates are invented, so treat these as estimates, not measurements.

What this is

Smart-charging and flexibility providers rarely control an EV directly. They utilise each car or charger maker's cloud platform to send start and stop commands, and those integrations can be slow, drop commands or let cars go to sleep. This project puts a price on that unreliability: in pounds, in cars not charged in time, and in the extra traffic needed to work around it.

  1. Step 1

    Pick the cheapest hours

    Every evening, software picks the cheapest half-hours to charge each car, using real Octopus Agile electricity prices for London (September 2026).

  2. Step 2

    Send start and stop messages

    Through the night it sends "start charging" and "stop charging" to the car or charger maker's system, then checks the car did what it was told.

  3. Step 3

    Things go wrong

    Those systems can be slow, ignore messages, send wrong updates, or let cars fall asleep. We measured how much each problem costs, and how hard the software has to work around it.

Everything except the electricity prices is simulated: 5,000 cars, four made-up companies whose failure rates we invented, and control software written for this project. No real car or company was involved.

What a partner API should guarantee

A minimum integration standard to put to car and charger makers, with each requirement priced by the value it protects (per car per month) and the extra command and polling traffic it avoids each night. "Slider test" figures come from the explorer above; "one-off test" figures price a single failure mode at one assumed severity, so read them as indicative.

  1. Report each charger's real charging speed

    Worth £0.86 per car per month when 15% of chargers claim 7 kW but really deliver 3.6 kW. 17.7 cars per 1,000 were not charged in time each month; 17.3 of those were our software's fault (it could have learned the real speed from meter readings), so part of this price is ours.

    Messages: no changeFrom the one-off test

  2. Respond quickly: typically within 10 seconds

    A slow partner costs £0.025 at 10 s, £0.085 at 30 s and £0.20 at 70 s (per car per month). From 70 s some cars start missing their charge (4 of 20 settings); at 150 s, 19 of 20 settings do.

    Messages: about 4.7 fewer per car per night (2 s vs 30 s)From the slider test

  3. Publish maintenance times, and keep them out of the 4am to 7:30am departure rush

    A daily unannounced 1-hour outage costs up to £0.99 per affected car per month (Kestrel-like) or £1.03 (Halcyon-like), and 69 cars per 1,000 were not charged in time each month. A published calendar only helps if the control software uses it well; ours did not fully.

    Messages: 10.4 / 5.9 extra per car per nightFrom the one-off test

  4. Wake sleeping cars reliably: within 60 seconds, and keep them awake for 10 minutes

    If cars fall asleep the way we assumed, any amount of sleep adds £0.034 to £0.039 per car per month. Removing sleep saves £0.033 to £0.034 per car; heavy sleep with lost wake-ups cost £0.16 per car (Kestrel-like).

    Messages: about 4.9 fewer per car per nightFrom the slider test and one-off tests

  5. Stick to the message limits you publish, with room for every car at the cheapest half-hour

    £0.17 per Bramble car with slow links and a tight limit; £0.013 per car when the real limit is half the published one.

    Messages: 1.0 to 1.9 extra per car per nightFrom the one-off test

  6. When you say a command worked, it really worked

    Under £0.01 per car per month at every rate of ignored messages we tried, because the software checks and resends.

    Messages: up to 2.8 extra per car per nightFrom the slider test

  7. Status data that is honest and up to date

    £0.004 per car per month or less, because the software double-checks.

    Messages: up to 2.5 extra per car per nightFrom the one-off test

  8. Status updates you can trust: the right type, in the right order

    £0.006 per car per month or less, because the software double-checks.

    Messages: 4.0 fewer per car per night (Halcyon-like)From the one-off test

  9. Accurate clocks: timestamps within 5 seconds

    £0.006 per car per month or less.

    Messages: up to 4.8 extra per car per nightFrom the one-off test

  10. A meter reading for every car, every night, at a known time

    About zero in charging value (£0.0001), but it is what makes the bill accurate when other data goes wrong.

    Messages: 0.93 extra per car per night if 10% are missingFrom the one-off test

  11. Commands that are safe to send twice

    Zero measured (at most £0.0001), because a repeated start or stop does no harm here. It is what makes all the resending above safe.

    Messages: makes items 2, 4 and 6 possibleFrom the one-off test

Most problems cost pennies because the software works around them by sending more messages. That extra work, and the risk of hitting the partner's message limits, is the hidden cost. Costs per car were higher with 5,000 cars than with 1,000 (£0.106 against £0.044 for the same set-up), possibly because message limits start to bite (a guess; no limit was actually hit), so these figures may be underestimates.

Where it goes wrong

Limitations

What this does not show.

  • The failure rates are made up. We don't know how real car and charger companies behave. A finding like "sleeping cars were hardest" only holds if cars sleep the way we assumed.
  • One month, one place. September 2026 prices for London. Winter, other regions and clock-change nights aren't covered.
  • Simplified money. The figures are estimates of savings, not real bills. Home car charging only; none of the other ways companies get paid for shifting electricity use.
  • The plan is fixed each evening. Real systems re-plan overnight, which would recover some of the cost of slow systems.
  • The control software is a stand-in, and its testing was stopped early. After 4 rounds of stress-testing, some problems were still unfixed: chargers that claim 7 kW but deliver less, unannounced maintenance in the morning rush, poor use of published maintenance calendars, and rare clock problems. Some explorer settings are flagged for this.
  • Judgement calls in how failures were counted. Cars got a 5-minute grace period (300 s) after a partner's outage before a miss counted against the software; extra charging caused by a partner's outage counts as a warning, not a failure; a missed charge is priced at the highest price in the next 24 hours; and for slow chargers, getting the car charged beat sticking to the plan.
  • Size. Most tests used 1,000 cars; the headline uses 5,000. Costs per car were higher at 5,000, so the smaller-test figures are probably underestimates.

How the numbers get here: every figure on this page comes from the project's results files, produced by simulation runs and checked by independent tests. The explorer, headline and company cards are looked up and never re-simulated. The guarantee figures are computed at build time from the same results. Company names are made up. Prices: Octopus Agile (London) and Elexon, September 2026.
